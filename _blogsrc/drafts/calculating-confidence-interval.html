Statistics is an important field for understanding machine learning in depth. I've decided to produce a series of posts to cover some of the basic concepts in simple way from the ground up.

Intuition

What does CI mean?

A confidence interval is an interval in which a measurement or trial falls corresponding to a given probability. In statistics, confidence intervals centered about an estimate of the mean. Because these confidence intervals rely on the standard error of the estimate, they increase as sample size decreases and they decrease as sample size increases. Consequently, the confidence interval, in statistics, is often a margin of error for the estimate of the mean.

A 95% confidence interval is a range of values that you can be 95% certain contains the true mean of the population. This is not the same as a range that contains 95% of the values. The graph below emphasizes this distinction.

Don't view a confidence interval and misinterpret it as the range that contains 95% of the values.

Let's rephrase this

Does this mean that we can say there is a 95% chance that the confidence interval you calculated contains the true population mean. 

*Yes, we can*

Can we also say that there is a 95% chance that the population mean lies within the interval?

The population mean has one value. You don't know what it is. If you repeated the experiment, that value wouldn't change. Therefore it isn't strictly correct to ask about the probability that the population mean lies within a certain range.

One more example

Let's suppose we are assigned a task to understand more about all men heights in the world. What should we do?

We wish to measure the height of all men in the world but this is impossible. So we will take a sample of 40 randomly chosen men and get the mean of their height.

Mean = 175 cm
Std = 20 cm

The 95% Confidence Interval (we show how to calculate it later) is:

175cm ± 6.2cm

This says the true mean of ALL men (if we could measure all their heights) is likely to be between 168.8cm and 181.2cm.

But it might not be!

The "95%" says that 95% of experiments like we just did will include the true mean, but 5% won't.

So there is a 1-in-20 chance (5%) that our Confidence Interval does NOT include the true mean.

But how can we know if we are lucky and the CI contains our mean or not?

Unless we get to measure the whole population (in the above case, it isn't possible but in some other cases it could be possible) we simply don't know.

This is the risk in sampling, we might have a bad sample.

Inferring the equation

So our target is to know some true population parameter (the average height of men in US or the proportion of voters who said yes) which can be estimated by a random sample and then we look at the sample parameter.

The confidence interval is range that will overlap with true population propotation 95% of the time if our confidence target is 95% for example. So the range will be
statistics value ± expected error

How to calculate it?

CI = Z-score * (Variance/N)^0.5

So for 95% confidence, and assuming that our sample size is larger than 30 which can be modeled as gaussian distribution, we will use z score measure.

z-score corresponding to 95% = 1.96

If the sample size is smaller than 30, it would be modeled as t-distribution and instead of z-score we will use the t-value.

t-value will depend on the degrees of freedom (df) as a reflection of sample size. When using the t-distribution to compute a confidence interval, df = n-1.

t-distribution is a type of probability distribution that is similar to the normal distribution with its bell shape but has heavier tails. As the sample size gets larger, the t-distribution gets more similar to standard normal distribution.

Validity of CI

In order for us to be confident about our confidence interval we need to validate few conditions:

- Our samples are random
- Biased samples lead to inaccurate results, so they shouldn't be used to create confidence intervals or carry out significance tests.

- The normal condition
- The distribution of sampling proportion p^ is approximately normal as long as the expected number of successes and failures are both at least 10. This happens when our sample size nnn is reasonably large.
- So we need: expected successes: np≥10 and expected failures: n(1−p)≥10
If we are building a confidence interval, we don't have a value of p to plug in, so we instead count the observed number of successes and failures in the sample data to make sure they are both at least 10. If we are doing a significance test, we use our sample size n and the hypothesized value of p to calculate our expected numbers of successes and failures.

- Independence condition
- In case we are sampling without replacements, the sample size shall be less than 10% of the population size.
